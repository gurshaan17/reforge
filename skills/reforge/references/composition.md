# Composition

Choose structure from the visitor's task and the available evidence. Preserve established brand identity, content, and imagery; layout freedom does not authorize replacing them.

## Choose the organizing idea

Identify what the visitor must understand or do next, then choose the structure that makes that easiest:

| Content need | Useful organizing approach | Decision check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Understand a product through its interface | Give the demonstration enough space to explain the promise; keep explanatory text close to the relevant detail. | Can the visitor recognize what the product does at the rendered image size? |
| Follow an editorial story | Use a deliberate reading sequence with varied emphasis, meaningful headings, and imagery at the points it explains. | Does each section advance understanding rather than repeat the promise? |
| Compare options | Align equivalent attributes, make differences scannable, and keep selection actions associated with their option. | Can visitors compare the information they need without remembering distant sections? |
| Complete a task | Center the form or action, with supporting explanations and recovery nearby. | Is the next action clear in normal, incomplete, and error states? |

These are organizing approaches, not required templates. Combine them only where the content changes purpose. Use a split hero or a card grid when the relationship between the content warrants it; do not impose either on every page.

## Establish hierarchy and rhythm

Choose the main focal point for each region and make surrounding elements support it through scale, contrast, alignment, and space. Keep the primary action easy to find without making every link equally prominent.

Use shared alignment lines and a coherent spacing scale across sections. Group related content more closely than unrelated content. Vary density when the story needs a change of pace: a spacious introduction can lead into detailed evidence, followed by a concise action. Avoid mechanically alternating layouts or wrapping every item in a bordered card.

Set image size according to what the image must communicate. A detailed interface needs more room than a decorative motif. Preserve the full subject and useful context; if an additional detail crop helps, retain access to the original view and avoid implying a feature the product does not have.

Keep a section when it answers a visitor question, provides evidence, or supports an action. If existing sections repeat themselves, improve grouping and emphasis within scope rather than silently removing content.

## Compose for narrow screens

Decide the reading order before collapsing columns. Keep each claim with its evidence and each control with its label and help. Keep semantic source order consistent with the intended reading and keyboard order.

Reframe media for the available width without hiding meaningful imagery. If a product screenshot becomes illegible, consider a larger stacked presentation or an accessible way to inspect detail. Avoid simply shrinking the desktop composition. Comparison content may need repeated attribute labels to stay understandable when stacked.

Let real content determine where the layout changes. Check intermediate widths as well as desktop and mobile endpoints; long headings and translated or enlarged text can expose failures between breakpoints.

## Inspect the composition

Review the full page for reading sequence, repeated emphasis, excessive gaps, cramped regions, and abrupt changes in visual language. Then inspect the focal region at actual reading size: can the product, evidence, and action be understood without relying on the design rationale?

Tie corrections to the working design brief. If the primary action disappears among equally prominent controls, adjust that hierarchy and re-render. If imagery dominates without explaining the product, improve its framing and adjacent explanation. Stop when the observed problem is resolved and surrounding content still works.
